# <span class="masthead-store">Amazon</span> Return Policy: How Long Do You Have, and Who Decides?
{: .masthead}

Amazon's 30 days are counted from the day the parcel is delivered, not the day you ordered. That part is simple. The part that catches people out is that 30 days is a default rather than a promise, and the item you bought may not have it.

## The clock starts at delivery

If you ordered on the 1st and the parcel arrived on the 5th, the window runs from the 5th. Counting from the order confirmation makes your window look shorter than it is, so use the delivery notification rather than the receipt.

This is the same way [H&M]({{ '/return-policies/hm/' | relative_url }}) counts, and the opposite of [Zara]({{ '/return-policies/zara/' | relative_url }}), which starts its 30 days when the parcel ships rather than when it lands.

## Thirty days is a default, not a promise

Amazon sets 30 days as the standard, but individual listings can differ. Some categories are shorter, some items cannot be returned at all, and anything marked as a final sale is out from the start.

The reliable move is not to count. Amazon shows the return eligibility on the product page before you buy, and the actual return deadline against the order in your order history afterwards. That date is the one that counts.

## Who sold it changes who handles it

| What the listing says | Who handles the return | What applies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ships from and sold by Amazon | Amazon | Amazon's standard policy |
| Sold by a seller, fulfilled by Amazon | Amazon | Amazon's standard policy in most cases |
| Sold and shipped by a seller | The seller | The seller's own policy, which Amazon requires to be at least 30 days |

The third row is where the surprises live. The window is still at least 30 days, but the process, the address the parcel goes to and any fee are the seller's, not Amazon's.

## If a seller will not play fair

Amazon's A-to-z Guarantee is the backstop. If a seller refuses a return that should be allowed, or does not respond, you can raise a claim through your orders and Amazon reviews it. That takes time, which is another reason not to leave a return until the final day.

## Holiday returns are extended, but announced each year

Amazon has run an extended window for several years, letting purchases made in November and December go back into the following January. The exact dates are published each season rather than being a permanent rule, and some brands are excluded or given an earlier cut-off. Check the dates for the current season rather than assuming last year's apply.

## What cannot go back

Items marked Final Sale are non-returnable and non-refundable. Beyond that the non-returnable list covers categories such as perishables, personalised items and digital content. Because the list changes, the returns and refunds section on the product page is the reliable source rather than any summary.

## Free returns, and the drop-off that is not

Many items qualify for free returns at drop-off points, often without needing a box or a printed label. Not every option is free for every order, and choosing a less convenient drop-off can attract a small charge that comes out of the refund. The returns flow shows the cost before you commit, so it is worth reading rather than clicking through.
