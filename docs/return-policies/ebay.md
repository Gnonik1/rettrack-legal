# <span class="masthead-store">eBay</span> Return Policy: The Seller Sets It, but You Are Covered Anyway
{: .masthead}

There is no eBay return policy. Every seller sets their own, and they range from no returns at all to sixty days. What most people miss is that a second set of rules runs underneath, and it applies even when the listing says returns are not accepted.

## Two clocks, running at once

The first clock is the seller's, and it covers changing your mind. The seller chooses the length when they create the listing: no returns, 14 days, 30 days or 60 days. It is printed on the listing page under the shipping estimate, and it is worth reading before you buy rather than after.

The second is the eBay Money Back Guarantee. It covers a much narrower set of problems, the item never arriving or not matching the listing, and it runs for 30 days from delivery. The seller cannot switch it off.

## Why that matters more than it sounds

A listing marked no returns accepted still leaves you covered if the item turns up broken, or is not what the listing described. That is a Money Back Guarantee case rather than a return, and the seller's own policy has no say in it.

The reverse catches people too. A seller offering 60 day returns has not extended the guarantee. If the item was misdescribed and you wait until day 45 because the listing said sixty, the guarantee window has already closed and you are relying on the seller's goodwill.

## When the guarantee clock starts

The 30 days run from the delivery date, using either the date tracking confirms it arrived or the latest date in the estimated delivery range shown at checkout, whichever of the two applies to your order.

That makes it a delivery-date clock like [Amazon]({{ '/return-policies/amazon/' | relative_url }}) and [Nike]({{ '/return-policies/nike/' | relative_url }}) rather than a shipping-date one, which is the more forgiving of the two.

## Who pays to send it back

This is decided by the reason, not by the seller's mood. If the item is faulty, damaged or does not match the listing, the seller covers return postage. If you have simply changed your mind, you pay it, unless the listing carries a free returns badge.

That badge is worth looking for. On a marketplace where the same product is sold by dozens of sellers, it is often the difference between two otherwise identical listings.

## Some listings carry a restocking fee

Sellers are allowed to charge one on remorse returns, and it comes out of your refund rather than being billed separately. Like everything else here it has to be stated in the listing, so the listing is the version that counts.

## What is not covered

The guarantee does not extend to everything sold on the platform. Digital content and intangible goods sit outside it, and so does anything where part of the payment happened away from eBay, such as a bank transfer or cash on collection. Paying outside the platform removes the protection entirely, which is the single most expensive mistake a buyer can make here.

## Keep the tracking

Send returns with a tracked service and keep the receipt until the refund has landed. On a marketplace the parcel is going to an individual rather than a warehouse, and tracking is what settles the question if the seller says it never arrived.

## When the money actually arrives

Once the seller has the item back they are expected to refund promptly, and eBay steps in if they do not. If the deadline passes with no refund, you can ask eBay to step in from the return request itself rather than waiting.

Where eBay resolves the case rather than the seller, the refund is issued by eBay and the seller is charged, so a seller who has gone quiet does not leave you without the money.

After that it is your bank, which is the same last stage as everywhere else on this site and the same one nobody controls.
