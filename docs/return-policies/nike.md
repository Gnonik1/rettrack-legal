# <span class="masthead-store">Nike</span> Return Policy: 60 Days, and You Can Actually Wear Them
{: .masthead}

Sixty days is double what most shops give you, but the more unusual part is what Nike lets you do with them. It calls the period a trial rather than a return window, which means you can actually wear the shoes before deciding.

## A trial, not a return window

Almost every other shop wants the item back unworn with the tags still on. Nike's own framing is that you should test whether the product works for you, so running in the shoes for a fortnight and then sending them back is within the spirit of the policy rather than a loophole.

That is a genuinely different deal from [Zara]({{ '/return-policies/zara/' | relative_url }}), [H&M]({{ '/return-policies/hm/' | relative_url }}) or [Shein]({{ '/return-policies/shein/' | relative_url }}), where a worn item is refused at the counter.

It is a trial, not an unlimited one. Nike keeps a record of return activity and can refuse returns it considers excessive, so this is a policy to use rather than to farm.

## Members return free, guests mostly do not

If you are a Nike member and were signed in when you bought, returning by post is free and you get a code that works without a printer. As a guest you generally pay the postage yourself.

There is a way around that which costs nothing: guests can take an eligible item into a Nike store and return it there for free, including things bought online. If there is a Nike store you pass anyway, that is the cheaper route.

## When the clock starts

For an online order the 60 days run from delivery. For something bought in a Nike store they run from the date on the receipt. Membership also matters here for a second reason: if you were signed in, your purchase history acts as the receipt, whereas a guest without a paper receipt may be offered store credit at the current price rather than a refund.

## Clearance stores are a dead end

Anything bought at a Nike Clearance Store cannot be returned at all, and a Clearance Store will not accept returns of items bought anywhere else either. A handful of other things sit outside the 60 days, including gift cards, certain custom jersey products and some sealed or third-party items, so it is worth checking the product page on anything unusual.

## The warranty clock is not the purchase date

Separately from the 60 days, Nike will look at shoes and kit that turn out to be faulty, generally for two years from the manufacture date printed on the product tag rather than from the day you bought them.

That distinction quietly costs people cover. A pair that sat in a warehouse for eight months arrives with eight months of its warranty already spent, and nothing on the receipt tells you so.

## Bought it somewhere else?

Nike's policy only covers what you bought from Nike, whether that is the website, the app or a Nike-owned store. A pair of Nike trainers bought from a department store or another retailer has to go back to that retailer under their rules, not these ones.

## Do not sit on the return label

Once you start a return and generate the label or code, it has its own expiry, which is shorter than the 60 days and is shown to you when you create it. Starting the return early and then leaving the parcel in the hall is a way to miss a deadline you thought you had already met.

## When the money actually arrives

Taking the item into a Nike store is the fastest route by a distance, because it removes the parcel and the inspection from the process entirely. The refund is authorised on the spot and after that it is only your bank you are waiting on.

A posted return has to travel, arrive and be checked before anything is issued, which is why the same return can feel weeks apart depending only on how you sent it back.

If you exchanged rather than refunded, nothing comes back at all, so it is worth being clear which of the two you asked for when you started.
